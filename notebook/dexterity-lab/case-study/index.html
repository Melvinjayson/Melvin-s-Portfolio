Dexterity Lab:

How do you make a procedural 3D task understandable beyond watching an animation? I explored how task selection, time controls, visible manipulation and feedback can help people inspect what a scripted system is doing.

01 / The context

The challenge isn't just rendering a hand.

A 3D movement can look convincing without telling a viewer much about what happened. Which task is active? What changed? Can someone revisit a particular step, inspect it from another angle or distinguish the displayed data from a physical measurement?

Dexterity Lab is a portfolio experiment in making those states accessible through interface design. The initial prototype uses simplified hands, a dark workbench, wooden blocks and coloured cups. It wasn't commissioned by an identified client, and no user population or validated educational outcome has been established.

02 / The implementation

Small procedural rules, visible states.

The current code generates simple Three.js geometry for a tower, cube and cup arrangement. It defines named operations and computes object positions from the current time. This keeps replay, scrubbing and stepping tied to the same time parameter rather than relying on a recorded video.

Jenga has three scripted extraction intervals and a later disturbance-and-collapse animation. The Cube sequence raises and rotates an object. Cups uses timed positioning to construct a five-level pyramid. The hands and arms are geometric approximations; the implementation does not include a robotic kinematic chain or physical grasp constraints.

03 / Interaction decisions

Turn a demonstration into something inspectable.

Task tabs help establish context. Operation buttons and object-selection controls offer shortcuts to relevant moments. The timeline allows someone to pause, step, scrub and change speed; camera presets make it possible to examine a sequence from multiple perspectives.

The design trade-off is important. Deterministic scripted motion is straightforward to replay and examine, but doesn't prove that a physical manipulator could perform the task. A physically grounded system would require verified joint definitions, collision handling, constraints and real or simulated data.

04 / What I can demonstrate

Behaviour is visible. Performance isn't proven.

The implementation establishes an interactive procedural visualisation with three task modes and shared playback controls. It shows the feasibility of the interface pattern—not improved learning, physically accurate manipulation or successful robotic execution.

Evidence boundary: The displayed wrist indicators are generated by procedural expressions; force and stability values are illustrative. No calibrated motion-capture data, physics solver, external validation or formal user testing is documented in the repository.

The next valuable step is not another more polished render. It is making the underlying states and measurements trustworthy: explicit task targets, true kinematic relationships, accessible selection, meaningful feedback and a documented method for evaluating whether people understand what they see.

05 / Visual language

Symbols should carry meaning.

For the case study, I use a restrained set of process metaphors: a rule or sequence, a target and a repeated loop. These are section markers and explanatory cues, not claims that a procedural node editor or deformable mesh engine exists in the current prototype.

Rule / state

One input produces a visible transition.

Target / control

Identify the intended position or operation.

Iteration / replay

Return to a step and examine it again.

Supporting evidence / Expand for details

What I know, and what needs verifying.

Confirmed by the current code

Three.js is imported from a CDN. There are Jenga, Cube and Cups modes, scene geometry, scripted operations, a timeline, playback speed selection, camera presets and simplified indicators. Jenga constructs 54 blocks in 18 layers; Cups constructs 15 cups.

Not established by this source

Original client or educational brief, collaborators, research sessions, user testing, a real learner population, physically grounded trajectories, force or stability measurements, validated outcomes or a delivered robotics product.

What I would investigate next
  • Who needs to inspect a manipulation sequence, and what decision are they making?
  • Can the viewer identify the selected object, state and next step without guidance?
  • How should actual joint and motion data be represented and verified?
  • Would a physics-based replay materially improve understanding, or add distracting complexity?
Reflection

The design lesson is in the handoff.

Making something move is one part of building a 3D interaction. Making that movement understandable is a separate design problem. This experiment gives me a practical setting to examine the relationship between procedural rules, interaction controls and feedback.

For the portfolio, that is the story worth showing: not the appearance of technical sophistication, but the effort to make a complex system inspectable—and being clear about what remains unresolved.
